# 2026-09-05

## 1. Business performance

The last completed week brought in **€73.03**, the fourth weekly rise in a row
and our strongest week yet. The more important number sits underneath it:
**thirteen separate buyers paid us that week, against five the week before, and
the largest buyer's share of revenue fell from 96% to 76%.**

Every week this summer, one wallet was effectively the whole business. That has
begun to change, and it changed while the total was still climbing — we added
buyers without losing the big one. It is not won: our bar for a healthy book is
no single buyer above 60%, and losing today's largest would still take most of
the income with it.

**Revenue is still growing, and for the first time the growth is not all coming
from the same buyer.**

## 2. What materially changed

**Our largest customer brought us new work and we turned four fifths of it
away.** On 24 August they ran nearly two hundred German company lookups in one
day — every one an insurance broker, plainly working down a list. We answered 36
and refused 159. In the twelve days since, they have not attempted a single
German lookup, while still buying 160 to 320 of everything else from us daily.
German lookup is the only thing in their basket that fails; the rest runs at or
near 100%. Nobody was charged for a refusal — it cost us no money, only the
work.

**The refusals were right; the way we deliver them is wrong.** When two
separately registered German companies match a name equally well, we refuse
rather than pick one — handing back the wrong legal entity is the worst thing a
company-data service can do. But we already know which two matched, and we bury
that in an English sentence rather than returning it in a form the customer's
software can use. Their system asked a question and got "no", where it could
have got "here are your two candidates, which did you mean". Correct judgement,
useless packaging.

German lookups are also switched off until tomorrow evening — our German data
supplier's monthly free allowance ran out and renews on its own. That happened
the day *after* the customer stopped, so it is not what lost us the work.

## 3. Fixed automatically

**Yesterday's improvements had never actually reached customers.** They sat
finished but unreleased, blocked by a safety check — which was right to block
them. A new tool, built to stop us mistaking our own automated testing for real
customer activity, could reach production over a connection able to change data
while promising it only ever looked. The one tool meant to keep us honest about
our numbers would have been the one able to alter them. It can no longer write,
it now works at all (it did not), and yesterday's work is live.

## 4. Working on now

**Win back the German work our largest customer walked away from** — return the
choices we already have instead of a refusal their software cannot act on. It
cannot be proven end to end until our supplier's allowance renews tomorrow
evening.

**Find out where the new buyers came from**, so the fall in concentration is
something we can repeat rather than something we noticed.

**Stop scoring "we declined" as "we broke".** Across last month's customer
buying, roughly one euro of demand was refused for every seventeen earned, and
every refusal I examined was correct — including fourteen requests for a review
site whose terms forbid us reading it automatically. Both kinds are subtracted
from the same health score, which is how a service doing exactly its job came
close to being switched off. Separating them also turns refusals into what they
are: customers telling us what they want to buy next.
